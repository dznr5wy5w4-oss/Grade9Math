Interactive study guide for the seven HRCE units, aligned to the 2022 Mathematics 9 outcomes. Formula sheet, worked examples, practice with hints, drill mode and four sample tests. Your progress saves in this browser.

The year on a number line

Seven units carry the course. Units 7 and 8 (Similarity and Transformations, Circle Geometry) were removed from Mathematics 9 as non-foundational, so HRCE keeps the original numbering and the year jumps from Unit 6 to Unit 9.

What you are walking into

Mathematics 9 has no provincial exam in Nova Scotia. Your mark comes from your school’s assessments across the year: unit tests, assignments and a final set by your teacher. That changes the strategy. Instead of one June cram, the payoff is in keeping each unit solid as you go, because Units 4 to 6 build directly on Units 1 to 3, and Mathematics 10 (which does have a provincial exam worth 20%) assumes all of it.

Verify the weighting of tests and assignments with your teacher; it varies by school.

Where marks get lost

Six of the Mathematics 9 performance indicators ask you to find the error in someone else’s work. That is the curriculum telling you where errors are common. The Watch spots tab covers all six.

Watch spots

Six of the Mathematics 9 performance indicators ask you to identify an error in a worked solution: N02.05, N04.03, N05.03, PR03.04, PR06.06 and PR07.05. Those are the places the curriculum expects mistakes. Here is each one, the mistake, and the fix.

1. Exponent laws applied to the wrong thing N02.05

The mistake: 3² × 3³ = 9⁵, or 2³ + 2² = 2⁵.

The fix: The product law keeps the base and adds exponents: 3² × 3³ = 3⁵. It only works when the bases are the same and the operation is multiplication. There is no law for adding powers; evaluate each one first: 2³ + 2² = 8 + 4 = 12.

Related trap: (−2)⁴ = 16 but −2⁴ = −16. Without brackets the exponent applies to 2 only, then the negative is put on at the end.

2. Order of operations, especially with negatives N04.03

The mistake: −3 + 2 × 5 = −5 (adding first), or (−6)² ÷ 4 − 1 = 36 ÷ 3 (subtracting before dividing).

The fix: Brackets, exponents, then multiply and divide left to right, then add and subtract left to right. −3 + 2 × 5 = −3 + 10 = 7. (−6)² ÷ 4 − 1 = 36 ÷ 4 − 1 = 9 − 1 = 8.

Related trap: Subtracting a negative. 2.4 − (−1.7) = 2.4 + 1.7 = 4.1.

3. Square roots of decimals N05.03

The mistake: √6.4 = 3.2, because “half of 6.4 is 3.2.” Or √0.4 = 0.2.

The fix: Square roots are not halves. Check by squaring: 3.2² = 10.24, not 6.4. Write decimals as fractions: 0.4 = 40/100 and 40 is not a perfect square, so 0.4 has no exact root. 0.49 = 49/100, so √0.49 = 7/10 = 0.7.

4. Solving equations: undoing in the wrong direction PR03.04

The mistake: 2x + 3 = 11, so 2x = 14, x = 7 (adding 3 instead of subtracting). Or 2(x + 3) = 14 becoming 2x + 3 = 14 (forgetting to distribute to both terms).

The fix: Whatever is done to x, do the opposite to both sides. 2x + 3 = 11, 2x = 8, x = 4. Then verify: 2(4) + 3 = 11 ✓. Verification catches nearly every one of these.

Inequality trap: −2x > 6 gives x < −3. Dividing by a negative flips the sign. Test x = −4: −2(−4) = 8 > 6 ✓.

5. Subtracting polynomials PR06.06

The mistake: (3x + 2) − (x − 5) = 2x − 3. The negative was applied to x but not to −5.

The fix: Subtracting a polynomial means adding the opposite of every term: (3x + 2) + (−x + 5) = 2x + 7. With tiles, flip every tile of the second polynomial before combining.

Related trap: Combining unlike terms. 5x² + 2x is already simplified; it is not 7x³.

6. Multiplying or dividing polynomials by a monomial PR07.05

The mistake: 2x(3x − 5) = 6x − 10 (forgetting the exponent), or (8x² − 12x) ÷ 4x = 2x − 12x (dividing only the first term).

The fix: Every term gets multiplied or divided. Multiply coefficients and add exponents: 2x · 3x = 6x². Divide coefficients and subtract exponents: 12x ÷ 4x = 3. So 2x(3x − 5) = 6x² − 10x and (8x² − 12x) ÷ 4x = 2x − 3.

Test tips

The week before a unit test

  • Read the unit’s lessons in Textbook, answer every check, then do every Practice question for that unit and the unit test in Sample tests. Empty your Drill set before test day.
  • Redo the questions you got wrong on the last quiz or assignment. Tests are built from the same indicators.
  • Do the unit’s Show your work problem on paper and compare your steps to the solution, not just the answer.

During the test

  • Read every question twice. Misreading “evaluate” as “simplify,” or missing a negative sign, costs more marks than not knowing the math.
  • Brackets first, always. Before you touch a power or an operation, look for brackets and decide what they include.
  • Verify by substitution. Every equation solution can be checked in under 30 seconds. Every inequality solution can be checked with two test values.
  • Show every step on constructed-response questions. Partial marks are real, and a clear process often earns most of them even when the last line is wrong.
  • Estimate first for square roots and surface area. If √50 comes out as 25 or a surface area comes out smaller than one face, stop and recheck.
  • If you get stuck, move on and come back. Do not spend ten minutes on one question when there are twenty more.

Calculator

  • A scientific calculator with brackets, an exponent key and a square root key. Not a graphing calculator unless your teacher says so.
  • Type brackets exactly as written. (−2)⁴ and −2⁴ give different answers on the calculator too.
  • For fractions, either use the fraction key or convert to decimals and back. Write the exact fraction in your final answer when the question uses fractions.

Common preventable mistakes

  • Adding exponents when adding powers (2³ + 2² is 12, not 2⁵)
  • Dropping the brackets on a negative base
  • Adding before multiplying in a mixed expression
  • Taking half instead of the square root
  • Forgetting to subtract the overlap (twice) in composite surface area
  • Distributing the negative to only the first term when subtracting polynomials
  • Dividing only the first term of a polynomial by the monomial
  • Not flipping the inequality sign after dividing by a negative
  • Answering the wrong question: solving for x when the question asked for the cost, the number of classes or the perimeter

Mindset

Mathematics 9 rewards steady work more than any single test. Every unit here feeds directly into Mathematics 10, and Mathematics 10 has a provincial exam. Treat each unit test as the rehearsal it is. If your heart is racing, two slow breaths, then start with the question you know best.
